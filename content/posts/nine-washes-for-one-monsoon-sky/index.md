Bangalore skies in July do a thing I have tried to paint for three years. The grey is secretly violet, and for about four minutes before dark the edges go gold. This is the ninth attempt, and the first one I didn't scrub off under the tap.

{{< img src="brush-jar.svg" treatment="inset-right" alt="Watercolour sketch of a jar holding a fat round brush and a flat hake" caption="A size 12 round and a flat hake" >}}

Watercolour skies are mostly about water. You decide how wet the paper is, then you get out of the way. I used {{< tangent side="left" style="paper" label="paper, briefly" note="Cold-pressed means the sheet was pressed between felts without heat, so it keeps its tooth. The tooth is what catches the violet when it granulates." >}}cold-pressed cotton paper{{< /tangent >}}, taped down on all four sides so it wouldn't buckle, and three pigments: a violet, a blue-grey and a warm ochre for the gold.

Brushes matter less than people think. A big round holds enough water to cover the sheet in a few strokes, and {{< tangent side="left" style="taped" label="word origin" note="Hake is simply the Japanese word for a brush. The wide flat ones are goat hair, soft enough to float water without scrubbing." >}}a flat hake{{< /tangent >}} lays the first even wash. Anything smaller and I start fiddling, and fiddling is how skies turn to mud.

The plan was three stages, each one started before the last had fully dried. Watercolour forgives a lot, but it does not forgive bad timing. Here is the sequence:

Step two is where the other eight attempts died. If the paper is too wet, the violet spreads into a flat purple. Too dry, and it sits on top in hard lines. The window is about {{< tangent side="right" style="sticky" arrow="loop" note="I timed it with the microwave clock. Ninety-two seconds, if you want to be smug about it." >}}ninety seconds{{< /tangent >}}, and you can tell by the shine: a soft sheen, no puddles.

{{< img src="ninth-wash.svg" treatment="breakout" alt="Watercolour of a violet monsoon sky over a dark ridge, with a gold band near the horizon" caption="Ninth wash, the full sheet, 56 x 24 cm" >}}

Up close it's messier than it looks from across the room. There's {{< tangent side="right" style="card" label="a word for it" note="Painters call it a backrun, or a cauliflower: fresh water creeping into a half-dry wash and pushing the pigment out into a frilly edge." >}}a bloom in the top left{{< /tangent >}} where water crept back into drying paint. Teachers call that a mistake. I'm keeping it.

For the record, this is roughly what I was looking at. The real thing was less purple and a lot louder; there was a pressure cooker going off two floors down.

{{< img src="balcony-reference.jpg" alt="Dusk over a dark ridge seen from a balcony, the sky fading from slate blue to peach" caption="The reference: my balcony at 6:40 pm, more or less" >}}

Nine washes, one sky, about forty minutes of actual painting spread over three years. The tenth sheet is already taped down.
